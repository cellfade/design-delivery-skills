# Narrative guide

Use this as a starting sequence, not a mandatory page template.

| Section role | Reader should understand | Prefer |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | What this is and why it matters | Short problem/outcome framing plus truthful product evidence |
| Problem framing | Who is involved, what blocks them and what is at stake | Users, relevant constraints, dependency/tension, guiding principles |
| Questions and assumptions | What is unknown and what the concept treats as true | Questions with investigation methods; explicit assumptions |
| Design process | How the designer arrived at the solution | Evidence → choice → consequence; rejected alternatives with reasons |
| Solution | How the selected experience works | Screens, a concise workflow artifact and interactive demo |
| Scope boundary | Where work stopped | Visible boundary; optional details for exclusions and exploratory ideas |
| Supporting artifacts | Where claims and process can be inspected | Source links, documents, design files, code and actual live links |

## Three reading depths

- **Quick scan:** headings alone identify problem, users, solution and scope.
- **Presentation:** a short sentence and a purposeful visual explain each key choice.
- **Inspection:** disclosures and links expose evidence, detailed flows and optional concepts.

## Copy checks

1. Does the headline state a problem or meaningful choice rather than a generic benefit?
2. Can someone unfamiliar with the product explain who acts and what changes?
3. Is each number attributed and used for an actual design implication?
4. Does the copy distinguish predicted benefits from measured results?
5. Is the same idea repeated in the hero, tension, principles, process and demo caption?
6. Are scope exclusions accompanied by a reason where they are discussed?
7. Can all optional content remain closed without hiding a critical caveat?
8. Does the material respect the submitted framing while clearly identifying later changes?

## Two useful comparisons

Compare the current version with a prior version for clarity and visual quality separately. A better prototype can still have a weaker explanation.

Compare references by section purpose, not surface decoration. A marketing homepage may teach hierarchy; a case study may teach reasoning. Borrow only the applicable lesson.
