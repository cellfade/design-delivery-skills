# Product case study

Make the design understandable to an unfamiliar audience. Treat the page as a visual aid with optional depth, not a transcript of every artifact created.

## Establish the story before styling

Read the source brief and the user's prior framing. Preserve distinctions between facts, assumptions, proposed research and observed results. Use supplied numbers when they materially explain a choice; do not manufacture opportunity estimates or treat scenario facts as independently verified industry claims.

Draft a short content outline using [the narrative guide](references/narrative-guide.md). Adapt the sequence to the audience and presentation format. The reader should understand the problem before encountering the questions or the prototype controls.

Separate:
- The design process: what informed the work, alternatives considered, choices and reasons.
- The proposed product workflow: how the designed experience behaves.
- The solution: the selected screens and interactive demonstration.

Show what was excluded and why. If exploratory prototypes exist, identify their limited fidelity and missing end-to-end work. Do not rewrite history to claim no exploration occurred or that scope labels prove compliance with the original timebox.

## Design the reading hierarchy

Use the user's references and relevant available design-taste skill to compare layout options by what each section needs to communicate. Preserve successful existing sections in a refinement.

Distinguish section roles through spacing, typography and composition before adding colored panels. A metric band and adjacent personas should not accidentally read as repeated equivalent feature cards. Use one focal statement for the main tension; keep supporting principles subordinate.

Every addition should replace redundant text or earn its place with new information. Use disclosure for secondary diagrams, research detail and future concepts when the primary story remains understandable while closed. Keep critical limits and scope boundaries visible.

Use plain actors and verbs: who sees what, who decides, what changes and what happens next. Simplification must preserve meaningful uncertainty and authority boundaries. Honor requests to propose copy before editing.

## Product evidence and imagery

Use verified UI captures for claims about designed behavior. Use imagery skills only when visuals add explanatory or presentation value. Preserve source UI accurately; generated scenery is not evidence of a working feature. Keep screen orientation, text contrast and composition consistent with user preferences.

Capture or synchronize design files after the relevant UI and copy stabilize. Record what is current and what lags. Do not claim design-file parity from an older screenshot or a contributor's unverified completion message.

## Review and deliver

Check the rendered page at the relevant viewport sizes, not source code alone. Inspect headline wrapping, reading order, screenshot legibility, disclosures, embedded prototype and artifact links. Copy review checks internal terminology, factual basis, reading load and statements of scope.

Trace evaluation criteria to visible evidence. Missing evidence calls for a disclosure or design decision, not an invented outcome. Provide a presentation route that fits the actual session segments, with optional depth reserved for questions.

Publish only within the user's authorization. Verify the final deployed page and sharing metadata when changed. Report specific checks and residual gaps without claiming exhaustive quality or guaranteed reviewer reactions.
